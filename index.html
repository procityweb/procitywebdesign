Having a Website Isn't the Same as Being Found

Many business owners are surprised to learn this: when a new website goes live, Google doesn't know it exists yet. Until Google understands what your site is about, it can't show it to anyone. It's like opening a lovely shop on a road with no sign. The shop is great, but nobody knows to stop in.

If you don't have a website yet, you can avoid this problem from the very start. If you already have one, this may be the reason your phone isn't ringing, and the good news is that it can be fixed.

Think about the last time you needed a business

Why does being found matter so much? Because you probably typed what you needed into Google, glanced at the first few results and called one of them. Most people never make it to page 2. Your future customers do the same thing, so showing up near the top really matters.

So how does Google decide who gets those top spots? Picture a friendly but careful judge. Before it recommends a business, it asks four questions:

  1. Does it clearly say who you are and what you do? Does the website tell your story and explain your services, whether you opened last month or have been at it for 20 years?
  2. Does it explain things clearly? Is there a page for each service someone might search for?
  3. Is it listed in the right places? Is the business found on other trusted websites and directories? We set these up for you, new business or not.
  4. Is it easy and safe to use? Is it secure, easy to read on a phone and simple to contact?

Many websites can't say "yes" to these questions, and that's usually why they never bring in customers. The good news: every website we build is made to say "yes" to all four, whether it's your very first website or a fresh start for an old one. But why do so many websites fail the test in the first place? It often comes down to how they're built.

Why so many websites fail the test

Why Websites Fail Google's Four Questions

Most websites don't fail because the owner did anything wrong. They fail because the website was built to look nice, and nobody set it up to pass the test. Here's how that usually plays out.

  1. 1
    "Who are you and what do you do?"

    The page says things like "Quality service you can trust," but never says what you actually do or where. Or it's only a business name and a phone number.

    For example: "Smith & Sons, Quality Service" leaves everyone guessing. "Smith & Sons: Roof Repair in Chicago" says it in one second.

    What it means: If a customer or Google can't tell what you do in a few seconds, they move on to someone who makes it clear.

  2. 2
    "Does it explain things clearly?"

    Everything you offer is squeezed onto one page. That page ends up being about everything in general, so it isn't the best match for any one thing a customer searches.

    For example: Someone searches "water heater repair." A plumber with a page only about water heater repair beats a plumber who lists 12 services in one paragraph.

    What it means: Google hands customers the page that matches their search best. No page for a service means no chance to win that search.

  3. 3
    "Is it listed in the right places?"

    The business only exists on its own website. It isn't on Google Maps, Yelp, the BBB or other trusted websites. (Those listings are called local citations.)

    For example: It's like applying for a job with no references. Nobody else is saying you're the real deal.

    What it means: When trusted websites list your business the same way, Google trusts you more. With none, Google has little reason to pick you.

  4. 4
    "Is it easy and safe to use?"

    The website is slow, the words are tiny on a phone, the buttons are hard to tap, or it's missing the little padlock (called SSL) that tells visitors it's safe.

    For example: A customer on their phone waits, pinches and zooms, gives up and calls the next business on the list.

    What it means: When visitors leave in seconds, you lose the customer, and Google tends to favor websites that are fast, safe and easy to use.

What is SEO?

SEO stands for search engine optimization. A search engine, like Google or Bing, is where people type what they need and get a list of businesses. SEO is everything done to help your business show up near the top of that list, like setting up your Google Maps listing, getting listed on trusted websites like Yelp and the BBB (called local citations) and making sure your website pages include the same words your customers type. Better SEO means more people find you instead of your competitors.
